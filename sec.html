English Conventions

These questions check punctuation and how the parts of a sentence fit together. Nearly all of them come back to one question: what is the punctuation job, and where is the main subject and finite verb phrase? Learn that first. Everything below uses it.

Start here

Is this group a complete sentence?

An independent clause can stand as a complete sentence. A dependent clause is attached to another part of a sentence and cannot stand as a complete sentence on its own. Either can have a subject and a finite verb.

  1. Find the main subject and whole finite verb phrase. The team won / is winning / has won / can win. In a command, you can be understood: Wait.
  2. Check whose verb it is. In the scientist who studied the reef, studied belongs to who; the whole group still lacks a main predicate. Add returned and the main clause is complete.
  3. Check its role. Is it a reason, condition or time clause; a description of a noun; or content inside another construction? Those are dependent uses. A sentence containing one can still be complete.
  4. Check required wording. The team placed… needs what was placed and where in this use. A subject and finite verb alone are not enough.
Three common dependent uses:
because the team won — reason/time/condition family;
who studied the reef — describes a noun;
that the result was reliable / what the team discovered — content in these uses. Each can contain a finite verb and remain dependent. These functional checks are more useful than memorising a supposedly complete word list.
Complete
The scientist who studied the reef returned.
The team won, though.
Not a complete sentence
Because the team won…
The long voyage
Studying the sample
A word is a clue, not a verdict. Though the team won… is dependent; The team won, though is complete. When can introduce a dependent time clause or occur in an independent question.

Do not call every incomplete group a dependent clause. The long voyage is a noun group. Studying the sample is non-finite; some grammars call such groups subordinate clauses. Neither stands as an independent sentence.

Look at the whole verb phrase: studying alone is non-finite, but is studying is finite. An -ing group can also be a subject: Keeping fruit fresh was difficult.

Ask yourself: “What is the main subject and finite verb phrase? Does this group stand alone, or does it function inside another construction?”

Try it: complete or not?
The forecast attempts to predict next month's weather.
Complete. Real verb: attempts (→ attempted). To predict can't show time, so it isn't the verb.
Attempting to predict next month's weather
Not complete. There's no real verb. Attempting and to predict can't show time on their own.
Keeping fresh fruit on long voyages was nearly impossible.
Complete. Real verb: was. The -ing phrase is the subject.
Although the crew stored the fruit in barrels
Not complete. It has a real verb (stored), but the hook word although leaves it hanging.
The scientist who studied the reef for ten years
Not complete. Studied belongs to the who part. The scientist never gets a verb of its own.
Joining sentences (Boundaries)

1Identify the job, then test the clauses

  1. Complex list? Group each whole item, including its internal names, descriptions and dates. Separate whole items with semicolons when they contain commas. Stop this branch here: the items need not be sentences.
  2. Paired interruption? Find where the removable description ends and close the printed comma, dash or bracket pair.
  3. Joining clauses? Check each group using the foundation above. Two independent clauses cannot be joined by a comma alone. Use a period, semicolon, or comma + an appropriate coordinating conjunction. An explanatory colon or dash can also work when the meaning and structure support it.
  4. Explanation or list after an introduction? A colon needs a complete introduction on the left and something that explains or lists it on the right.
  5. Dependent or supplementary group? A sentence-joining semicolon cannot attach it. Decide whether a comma or no mark fits its position. Do not split a subject from its predicate.
The museum opened in 1990; it now welcomes a million visitors a year. Left: real verb opened, complete. Right: real verb welcomes, complete. Two complete sides, so a semicolon (or a period).
Trap: a comma between two complete sides is the most common wrong answer on the whole test. It's called a comma splice.

2Signposts aren't glue

However, therefore, though, thus, instead, for example tell you how two ideas connect. They can't join two sentences. Use a valid clause boundary: a period or semicolon, a comma with a coordinating conjunction, or an explanatory colon or dash where the structure supports it.

Right
The plan sounded simple; however, it took four years.
The plan sounded simple, though; it took four years.
Wrong
The plan sounded simple, however, it took four years.
Two commas look tidy, but it's still a comma splice.

Ask yourself: "Where is the boundary?" Put the strong mark there. The signpost sits beside it, with a comma on its own side.

Watch though: at the start of a side it means although, a hook word, so that side is no longer complete.

3Colon or semicolon?

Colon :
Needs a complete sentence on the left. The right side can be anything that explains or lists what the left side promised.
The crew feared one thing: mold.
Semicolon ;
When joining clauses, needs an independent clause on both sides. In complex lists it separates whole items instead.
The crew feared mold; it ruined the flour.
Trap: if the left side ends on such as, including or like, it isn't complete, so no colon: "problems such as mold."

4Extra information comes in pairs

A description you could lift out, and still have a sentence, is set off by a matching pair: two commas, two dashes, or two brackets.

The festival—a month of music and film—drew visitors from four continents. It opened with a dash, so it closes with a dash, not a comma.

Ask yourself: "Did the sentence already open something?" Read to the end of the description before you choose.

5Lists whose items already have commas

If the items in a list contain commas (a city, its country), separate the items with semicolons. In this use, list items do not need to be complete sentences.

The tour visited Lima, Peru; Quito, Ecuador; and Bogotá, Colombia.
Trap: the two-sides test can't solve this one, because neither side is a sentence. Group whole items first, then match the separator between them. A printed semicolon is a clue; the item structure decides where it belongs.
Try it: pick the mark
The museum opened in 1990? it now welcomes a million visitors a year.
Semicolon. Both sides have a real verb and no hook word. A comma alone would be a splice.
Built in 1990? the museum now welcomes a million visitors a year.
Comma. The left side fails check 1: it has no subject. Nothing in it is doing the building, so it can't stand alone, and a semicolon is out.
The plan sounded simple? it took the team four years.
; however, Both sides are complete, and however is a signpost, not glue.
The tour visited Lima, Peru; Quito, Ecuador? and Bogotá, Colombia.
Semicolon. The sentence already printed one between items, so every item boundary takes one.
Matching the parts (Form, Structure, and Sense)

6Subject and verb agree

Find the real verb first, then ask who or what is doing it. One thing takes a singular verb, more than one takes a plural.

The list of names on the doors is out of date. Cross out "of names on the doors". The subject is list, not names or doors.
Trap: the noun right next to the verb is often not its subject. Long descriptions and which / who parts sit in between on purpose.

7Verb form: is the blank the real verb?

Inspect the particular clause or modifier position. Does its subject have a finite predicate yet? A sentence can contain several clauses or coordinated finite verbs; finding one verb elsewhere does not settle the blank.

No real verb yet
The blank is the real verb, so it needs a verb that shows time.
The scientists studied the samples.
Real verb already there
If the blank is inside a supplementary non-finite description, choose the required non-finite form. A different clause or a coordinated predicate can still require a finite verb.
The scientists, studying the samples, found a flaw.

Ask yourself: "Where is the real verb for this subject?" Choose the blank to fill whichever gap is left.

8Openers must touch what they describe

When a sentence opens with a modifier describing its main subject and a comma, the very next words must be the thing being described.

Right
Covered in snow, the trail was hard to follow.
Wrong
Covered in snow, the hikers found the trail hard to follow.
The hikers weren't covered in snow.
Trap: every wrong option is a perfectly good sentence on its own. Only the opener shows which one is right, so read it first and ask "who or what is this about?"

9Plurals and possessives

Each blank can hold two decisions: is it more than one, and does it own something?

the bird's nest (one bird) · the birds' nests (more than one bird) · the birds flew (no owning)
Owning
its · their · whose
No apostrophe for these.
Short forms
it's = it is · they're = they are · who's = who is

Ask yourself: "Could I say it is here?" If not, it's its.

10Pronouns point clearly and match

A pronoun must point to one noun and match it: singular with singular, plural with plural.

The company changed its logo. One company, so its, not their.

11Keep the tense steady

Stay in the tense the sentence starts in unless the timing really changes. For the earlier of two past events, use had + verb.

She opened the door and walked in. · By the time we arrived, the show had started.
Try it

is. The subject is list. "Of names on the doors" is in between.

studying. The real verb is already there (found), so the blank is extra.

The trail. It's the thing covered in snow, so it has to come right after the comma.

In three lines

  1. Find the main subject and whole finite verb phrase. Check its clause role and required wording.
  2. Punctuation: complex list or interruption first; then clause completeness and meaning. Adverbs do not supply a clause boundary.
  3. Matching: verb to its real subject, opener to what it describes, pronoun to its noun.
